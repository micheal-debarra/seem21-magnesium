Does intravenous magnesium reduce deaths after a heart attack?

In the 1980s several small randomised trials gave intravenous magnesium to people with a suspected heart attack (acute myocardial infarction). In 1991 Teo and colleagues pooled the seven trials then available, and the table starts with those seven. The outcome is death, mostly in hospital. The forest plot pools the trials in a fixed-effect meta-analysis. Tick or untick trials to include or leave them out, and use the form to add newer trials. About the trials

Results

Square: one trial (bigger = more weight). Line: its 95% CI. Diamond: pooled result. A risk ratio below 1 means fewer deaths with magnesium.

Dot: one trial, placed by its result (across) and its size (up). Diamond: the pooled result, placed at the total number of patients. Dashed line: no difference. Small trials scatter more widely by chance; if there is no bias, they should scatter evenly on both sides of the big trials, like an upside-down funnel.

How the numbers are worked out

Risk ratio: the risk of death with magnesium divided by the risk of death in the control group. Below 1 favours magnesium; 1 means no difference. Teo et al. reported an odds ratio (a 55% reduction in the odds of death), so their numbers differ slightly from these.

Pooling: a fixed-effect meta-analysis (inverse variance), the approach used in the original magnesium meta-analyses. It assumes that all the trials are estimating one true effect, so each trial is weighted by its precision and the largest trials dominate. A random-effects model, which allows the true effect to vary between trials, gives small trials relatively more weight. The diamond's centre is the pooled risk ratio and its width is the 95% confidence interval. P is the p-value for the pooled effect.

Chi² test (Cochran's Q): asks whether the trials differ more than chance alone would explain. It is weak when there are few trials, so a large P does not show that the trials agree.

I²: the share of the variation between trial results that reflects real differences rather than chance: I² = (Q − df) ÷ Q. Rough guide: 25% low, 50% moderate, 75% high.

Funnel plot: drawn as in Egger and Davey Smith (1995), with the number of patients on the vertical axis. Current guidance plots the standard error instead and advises formal tests for asymmetry only when there are at least 10 trials.

Risk of bias: our own ratings for this exercise, using the RoB 2 categories and the published reports. Reasons are under "About the trials".
